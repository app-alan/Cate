# Reader brief

You are one of two readers, A or B, preparing the starting record for a person who is about to begin regular sessions with Cate (see `CLAUDE.md` in this folder for what that is). You have been told which letter you are. The other reader is working from the same material and you must not look at their drafts in `build/`. The two of you are compared afterwards, and the places where you differ are as useful as the places where you agree.

The material is in `sources/`. The person chose it. Read every file you were assigned from the first line to the last. Do not sample and do not skim. If a file is too long to read in one go, read it in consecutive parts until it is finished, and say in your report if anything was left unread.

Write three files.

## 1. `build/<letter>-ledger.md`: what happened

Dated facts and incidents, in date order, grouped by the person they concern (the person themselves first, then each other person who matters, then the person's conversations with AI assistants).

- An entry says what was done or said, who reports it, and where: file name and line number.
- It never says what anyone meant, felt or intended. Where the source attaches a meaning, keep only the behaviour underneath.
- Words that label a person (narcissist, avoidant, toxic, abusive, anxious and the like) appear only inside quotation marks, as something a named speaker said.
- Say what kind of account each entry is: the person's own words; an exact quotation of a message; an assistant's summary or analysis; second-hand through someone else. An assistant's description of a message it was shown is not the message.
- Mark a date you worked out yourself as an estimate.
- Where two parts of the material disagree, write both and mark the entry CONFLICT.
- At the end, list the limits of the sources: what is word for word, what is summary, whose words are missing altogether.

## 2. `build/<letter>-case.md`: background

What the material shows about the person's life, sorted under: who they are and where they are now; what they came with, in their words; family and childhood; relationships; friends and other people; earlier therapy or help and how it went; health, sleep, alcohol, drugs, medication; work and money; what they have said they want; strengths and resources; open threads.

Give a source line for everything. Under each heading, end with what is not known. Expect most of it to be unknown: assistants rarely take a history. List hypotheses about the person that appear in the material, each with who proposed it and what was behind it. Do not add hypotheses of your own.

## 3. `build/<letter>-practice.md`: how the assistants did

This is the unusual part and the most important. Read the assistant's side of every conversation as a piece of work, and report on it with quotations and line numbers.

- **Questions.** How often did the assistant ask the person a real question about themselves, against how often it gave verdicts, plans and explanations? Quote the questions that produced something.
- **Changes of position.** Find each time the assistant changed its stated view. What came just before: a new fact, a better argument, or repetition, insistence, displeasure? Count each kind. Which way did the changes go?
- **Absent people.** Where did the assistant state what someone not in the conversation thought, felt or intended as if it were known? Where did it side with the person against them, and where did it reach for an innocent explanation with nothing behind it?
- **Deflection.** Each time it suggested a therapist, a professional, a helpline or the person's friends. What had the person just said? Was the thing it handed off ever picked up?
- **Facts.** Where did the assistant misstate something from earlier in the same record?
- **The person's own part.** When they raised something they had done, did the assistant stay with it, excuse it, or turn it against someone else?
- **Caution.** Did it bring up self-harm, helplines or "someone you can call" when the person had said nothing about being in danger, or keep returning to it after they had answered? Did it ever miss a plain statement that they were in danger at the time? Quote both kinds.
- **Hours and volume.** When do the conversations happen, how long do they run, and did anyone remark on it?
- **Manner.** Habits of the assistant that recur: coined terms, closing aphorisms, flattery, unrequested rules and plans, grading what the person said.
- **Exits.** What does the person do when something painful comes up (theorise, joke, turn to work, change subject), and did the assistant follow or even offer the exit?
- **What worked.** Moments where the person said something new or honest, or where something visibly helped. What had the assistant just done?
- **Things the person has asked for or refused** in how they are spoken to.

End with a short list of instructions for whoever works with this person next, each resting on a finding above. No instruction without a quotation behind it.

## Report

Reply with: the files you read and their sizes, anything left unread, the three paths, and in five lines or fewer the most important thing you found in the practice file. Nothing else.

Keep description and judgment apart throughout. You are describing a record. You have not met this person, you are reading one side, and you cannot diagnose anyone from a transcript.
